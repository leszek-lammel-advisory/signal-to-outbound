# From signal to message

These notes come from the outbound copy library we use for client campaigns. The short version: a message is worth reading when the reader gets something useful from it. A signal tells you when to write and what to offer. It does not tell you the reader has a problem.

- [signal-to-message-prompt.md](signal-to-message-prompt.md): a prompt that teaches your AI this method for your product.

## 1. Decide what you're giving them before you write the opener

Ask: what could this person do, understand or judge after my message that was harder before it? Say it in plain words first.

- An SDR ramp program: a practice call where the new rep pitches an AI playing one of the company's real buyers.
- An outbound agency: a list of 20 accounts in the new market, with why each one fits.
- A research firm: a sample of ten partner candidates, with the evidence behind each entry.

"Learn about our solution" and "a quick intro" are not contributions. They leave the reason to reply to the reader.

Two rules keep this honest:

- **"I have" or "I can prepare".** Say "I've put together" only if it exists. Otherwise "I can put together".
- **No useful contribution, no message.** Work on the offer first. A better opener won't fix an empty offer.

## 2. Read the signal: what happened, what it might mean, what you can help with

| | Example | In the message |
|---|---|---|
| **What happened** | They posted an SDR role for Munich. The posting says the rep will call data protection officers. | Say it. It's a fact they can check. |
| **What it might mean** | A new rep will need to learn how those buyers think. | Usually leave it out. They know what a new hire means. |
| **A diagnosis** | "Your onboarding can't keep up." | Never. You can't see it from outside. |

Find the work inside the event: a market, a responsibility, a product, a location, a deadline. A job ad that says "build our French MSP network" names a task. A job title alone doesn't.

Some events don't tell you which work they create. A new head of sales may be reviewing territories, inheriting accounts or changing nothing. Use that kind of event to decide **whom** to write to, and look for a firmer reason to write.

Keep what the source says. "Plans to open two offices" is not "opened two offices".

## 3. The signal pattern: change to practical help

The reader should recognise the work in front of them and see how you could help, while staying free to decide whether they need it. Recognition, not diagnosis.

```text
Hi {first name},

{the change, or the task they stated, specific enough to recognise}.

{what you can give them}, including {the detail that makes it useful to them}.

{the next step, naming what they get or decide}?
```

It fits Mateusz's two lines and a question. The order can flip when the offer is the stronger opening.

### Worked contrast (fictional)

**The brief.** LynxOps, an 80-person security software company, has a partner manager vacancy that names building reseller relationships with French managed service providers (MSPs). Claire leads partnerships. The sender sells market research and has finished a sample of ten French MSPs: their security services, current vendor partners and the public evidence behind each entry. No customer result is available. Nothing shows a pipeline problem or urgency.

**Weak:**

> Hi Claire,
>
> With LynxOps expanding its channel presence in France, scaling a strong partner ecosystem may be a priority. We help growing security companies accelerate partner development with targeted market intelligence.
>
> Are you exploring ways to improve partner acquisition? Happy to send a quick introduction.

What went wrong: the writer treats a vacancy as proof of a problem, hides the actual value behind "market intelligence", and asks Claire to supply the missing problem. "A quick introduction" is the sender's presentation, not something Claire can use.

**Stronger:**

> Hi Claire,
>
> Your partner manager advert mentions building a French MSP network. I've put together a sample of ten French MSPs showing their security services, current vendor partners and the evidence behind each entry.
>
> It could give you a starting list to compare with the firms you already know.
>
> Want me to send it?

The observation explains why this sample belongs in her inbox. The offer is concrete and checkable. Comparing it with her own list respects work she may already have done. Nothing is invented.

### The demo's version (fictional prospect)

Rampwise sells an SDR ramp program. Its free offer: a practice call for the new rep, with an AI playing one of the customer's real buyers.

> Hi Jonas,
>
> Your SDR posting for Munich says the new rep will call data protection officers at mid-sized companies.
>
> Before their first real call, I can set up a practice call where they pitch an AI playing one of those officers, built from your website and the posting.
>
> Want me to build it?

Line 1 uses the posting's own words: a task they stated. Line 2 says what Rampwise would prepare and for whom. It doesn't explain that new reps need training. The question names what Jonas gets.

## 4. The real reference: our best-performing first email

A first email from one of our new-hire campaigns, as sent, with the name removed. We count it as our best-performing copy flow so far. That's the owner's judgment about the flow, not a controlled test of this one email.

> Hi {first name}, how do government and defence teams usually become aware that they need an external partner for a secure digital transformation programme?
>
> I run outbound for B2B teams in and around IT and professional services. If I put together a demo campaign aimed at UK government and defence organisations entering the early planning stage of a major digital service, platform or operational-capability modernisation and deciding how to shape its user experience, architecture and secure delivery, would that be useful?
>
> P.S. I know it's super overused, but still - congrats for taking over growth ;)

Why it works:

- **The proposed work is visible.** The reader can picture a campaign aimed at a customer group they recognise, at a decision point they recognise.
- **It's about their customers, not their failings.** It doesn't claim they lack pipeline.
- **The offer carries the message.** "Would that be useful?" works only because it follows a concrete proposition. After "a two-minute demo of our platform" the same question would be empty.
- **The new role chose whom to write to.** It isn't the argument. The P.S. is incidental, not a rule to congratulate everyone.
- **It's long, and it worked.** Length isn't quality. A shorter version must keep the customer, the situation, the decision and the offer.

## 5. When the signal isn't the right lead: seven selling situations

Pick by what you actually have. No case study means no peer result. No useful asset means don't promise a guide: offer a quote, a sample, a check or a focused conversation instead.

| Situation | Shape |
|---|---|
| **A verified change creates work you can help with** | The change. What you can give them. The next step. (Section 3) |
| **A real comparable result makes you credible** | For {comparable customer}, we {documented result}. Why the comparison holds. Offer the detail. |
| **You found something useful they can check** | The finding, with enough detail to check. What it means. What else you have. |
| **A demo or sample can show one relevant job** | The job. I can {demonstration} for {their use}, showing {what they'll be able to judge}. |
| **They can judge a concrete commercial offer** | The offer. The availability, scope or terms that matter. A quote, shortlist or sample. |
| **You can contribute to a real decision** | The decision. A useful distinction you bring. One question whose answer changes the next step. |
| **A past exchange has a new reason to resume** | What you discussed. What changed. The next step on that decision. |

Patterns can combine when that makes one argument stronger, such as a change followed by a matching result. They are not seven messages to send.

## 6. Before you send: fix the earliest wrong decision

Read the full message as the buyer would. When something is off, the cause is usually earlier than the wording.

| What the reader sees | What went wrong earlier | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A detailed description of their company, then a pitch that would fit anyone | Research chosen for show, not relevance | Pick the fact that changes your offer, or drop it |
| "With your growth, you must be..." | A fact turned into a diagnosis | Go back to the fact. Offer help without claiming a problem |
| "Are you exploring...?" plus a demo ask | A question standing in for your contribution | Name the work your offer helps with |
| "Worth comparing notes?" | Nothing you're bringing | Say what you'd bring to the conversation |
| A list of features they have to decode | No recognisable moment of work | Show one person doing one job, and the result |

**The swap test.** Delete the signal sentence. If the rest reads exactly the same, the signal is decoration: go back to section 2. Then swap in another company with the same signal. If it still works, you have a template that scales.

**Rules we hold to:**

1. Open with "Hi {first name},".
2. End with a next step that names what they get or decide. Never "who owns this?", "is that hire on your team?", "worth a quick chat?" or "are you exploring...?".
3. One signal per message, said the way the source says it.
4. No invented facts, numbers or customer results.
5. No congratulations as the opener, no flattery.
6. On LinkedIn, aim for under 60 words after the greeting. Don't cut the link between the change and your offer to hit a number.
